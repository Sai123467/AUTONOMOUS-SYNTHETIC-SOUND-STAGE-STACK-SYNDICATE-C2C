Most game and film audio is recorded — a sample, played back on cue. Procedural audio is synthesized — built from mathematical building blocks (noise, oscillators, filters, envelopes) every single time it plays, so it can respond to a world that never repeats itself twice.

SIX DOMAINS

One idea, six different signals

The underlying method is the same — rules instead of recordings — but what feeds those rules, and what comes out the other end, changes completely depending on what's being described.

GROUND & FOOTSTEPS

Terrain that reacts underfoot

Every step is built, not fetched. A footstep engine takes the surface material, the character's weight, and their current speed as inputs, then synthesizes a burst of layered grains — short, randomized fragments of shaped noise — sized and pitched to match what's being walked on.

Gravel is many small, high-frequency grains fired in a loose cluster to mimic scattering stones. Snow adds a soft low-pass compression grain underneath, simulating crunch. Wood favors a single resonant impulse with a short decay, like a struck plank, while metal grating adds a ringing overtone tail generated with a lightly detuned oscillator.

Speed changes grain density and overlap; a sprint crowds grains closer together and raises their pitch slightly, the way a real footfall gets shorter and sharper. None of this touches a sample library — the same four or five parameters reshape the entire sound.

WEATHER & SEASONS

Storms, seasons, and the sound of a changing sky

Rain is rarely one recording — it's droplet density mapped onto layered noise bursts, with a separate, sparser layer for individual drops hitting specific surfaces (leaves, tin roofs, puddles) so a storm over a forest sounds different from one over a city.

Wind is pink noise pushed through a filter whose cutoff frequency rises and falls with wind speed, giving it that recognizable "gusting" motion. Thunder is a shaped noise transient with a randomized pitch, delay, and decay tail per strike, so no two claps of thunder are ever identical.

Seasons extend the same system over time instead of a single moment: a synthesis engine can slowly shift its own parameters across a calendar — crisper, higher-pitched wind and dry leaf-rustle grains for autumn; damped, muffled reflections and snow-crunch grains for winter; brighter, denser insect and bird-call layers as spring warms into summer.

EMOTIONAL SCORE

Music that tracks your pulse

Adaptive scores treat emotion as a set of live parameters rather than a fixed timeline: proximity to danger, remaining health, story tension, and pacing all feed a system that adjusts chord voicing, tempo, and instrumentation in real time as those values shift.

Two common approaches do this differently. Vertical remixing keeps a piece of music constant in tempo and key but mutes and unmutes instrument layers — strings for calm, percussion and brass stacked in as danger rises — so the transition feels continuous. Horizontal resequencing instead composes short musical phrases procedurally and stitches new ones together on the fly, so the melody itself evolves rather than just its layers.

A tense low string drone can be generated by detuning two oscillators slightly against each other; a "heartbeat" cue under fear is often a low sine pulse whose tempo is procedurally tied directly to a danger value. The listener never hears a loop end and another begin — only their situation changing.

IMPACT & COLLISION

Crashes with no source recording

Modal synthesis models an object's own resonant frequencies — a hollow bin, a glass pane, a steel bar — so a collision's force and material generate the impact sound directly from physics, not a sample library.

Two collisions of the same object rarely sound identical, because the physical inputs — force, angle, contact point — never are.

CREATURE & AMBIENCE

A world that breathes without looping

Creature calls combine formant synthesis and breath noise, with pitch contours tied to a creature's size and mood. Background ambience layers generative loops of birds, insects, and wind that are recombined so the pattern never audibly repeats.

Storage & Memory Footprint
Massive. Modern AAA games consume 20 GB to 60 GB purely for audio files, samples, and language variations.
Zero disk footprint. The entire audio engine lives in mathematical algorithms and code (< 500 KB total size).
Repetition & Auditory Fatigue
Pre-recorded files repeat the exact same sound byte every 15–30 seconds. Human ears naturally recognize repetitive loops, breaking immersion.
Infinite variation. Because white noise buffers and harmonic phases evolve over continuous mathematical curves, no two seconds of audio are ever identical.
Micro-Kinematic Reactivity
Cannot dynamically react to subtle movements. Looking up at the sky or down at your boots plays the exact same ambient track.
Continuous spatial DSP. Head tilt dynamically opens or closes physical low-pass filters in real time with sub-10ms response.
Emotional & Context Adaptability
Limited to cross-fading between a few pre-rendered stems (e.g., crossfading between calm.wav and combat.wav), which sounds artificial.
Zero-latency mood morphing. Natural language and emotional valence directly reshape musical frequencies, wave clipping, and resonance on the fly.
